Why we watch volcanoes breathe

Every volcano leaks gas, even while it sleeps. Reading that breath — continuously, is how NOVAC turns an invisible signal into an early warning.

Fascinating but dangerous

1 billion people live within 100 km of an active volcano, gas give important information that can save their lives and infrastructure.

Volcanoes leaks gas

Even a "sleeping" volcano rarely sleeps quietly — it keeps breathing out sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and other gases, like a slow, smoky sigh. Scientists get to know each volcano's normal breathing pattern. So when that pattern changes — a sudden gassy outburst, an unusual hiccup — it's their cue to sit up and pay attention, since it can be an early sign that an eruption is on the move.

A sudden rise is a warning sign

When magma pushes closer to the surface, gas emissions often spike days or weeks before an eruption — giving nearby communities precious time to prepare.

Gas clouds are a hazard too

Volcanic SO₂ and ash can damage aircraft engines and harm air quality far beyond the crater rim — pilots and health agencies rely on this data daily.

NOVAC

The goal of novac is to facilitate this data collection.

One global network, watching volcanoes' breath — so the next warning sign is never missed.
